Ray Peat on ageing

For Ray Peat, degenerative ageing isn't an inevitable effect of time passing, but something that happens according to circumstances, above all through a contamination of the cells' energy supply 1. He held that what limits us is our environment, not our genes, and that the environment can be changed 2. In his view, ageing goes hand in hand with an ever slower energy metabolism 3.

The cell's environment, more than its genes

According to Peat, the idea that we are defined and limited by our genes is being replaced by another: we are limited by our environment, and that environment can be changed. When we react to unsuitable environments, the internal medium becomes limiting for the cells, which, instead of renewing and repairing themselves, end up in dead ends 2.

That is why he rejected several classic explanations. He held that the theory of accumulated somatic mutations was shown to be false decades ago, and that in cells that become cancerous, functional changes destabilise the chromosomes, not the other way round 2. Nor did he accept that the Hayflick limit, of about 50 cell divisions, was a fixed quota: for him it is a product of the cell's environment 4. He saw some truth in the old idea that ageing is due to the loss of neurons, but considered it an oversimplification 4.

A metabolism that winds down

Peat explained that oxygen consumption per gram of body weight falls throughout life from the moment the fertilised egg gets going. Between puberty and old age it drops by around 70%, unless one intervenes by controlling inflammatory hormones and supporting thyroid function. According to him, the probability of dying grows in step with that loss of the capacity to burn energy 5.

He described ageing itself as a process of metabolic slowing from the earliest stages of life, so whatever curbs that slowing should lengthen life and support cognition 3. He added that older people oxidise fat just as young people do, but not sugar at the same rate, hence their lower overall metabolic rate 6. He also cited W. D. Denckla, for whom certain pituitary hormones speed up ageing by blocking the action of thyroid hormone and reducing oxygen consumption 7.

More metabolism doesn't mean more wear

Peat recalled that in his youth it was commonly believed that someone with a high metabolism would die young, like a candle that lasts less the brighter it burns, and that this image always bothered him 3. According to him, Weissman's wear-and-tear theory and Pearl's rate-of-living theory have been refuted by metabolic studies showing that more intense mitochondrial respiration reduces cell damage and favours a longer life 8.

In support he mentioned that small dogs eat much more in proportion to their size and sometimes live twice as long as large ones 8. He also cited hamsters with a 20% higher metabolism that lived 15% longer, and mice with a high metabolism that lived 36% longer than those with a low metabolism 8. Along the same lines, he pointed out that animals raised without bacteria have greater resistance to stress and a somewhat longer average lifespan 3.

Polyunsaturated fats, lipofuscin and ageing

Peat told how his interest in unsaturated fats was born while studying lipofuscin, the age pigment. According to him, it is a breakdown product of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA) that forms small brown clumps, like the age spots on the skin. He held that it consumes oxygen and wastes energy, and that it forms faster with stress and with excess estrogen 9. He described that pigment as a toxic material with proteins, cross-linked PUFA and metals, whose formation depends on the ratio of PUFA to saturated fats 10.

For him, with age the membranes of the mitochondria become more unsaturated, and so does the retina, which would thus be more exposed to light damage 11. He noted that long-lived species have less unsaturated fats and that calorie restriction reduces that accumulation 11. He added that PUFA inhibit hepatic glucuronidation, a detoxification system that weakens with the years, while oleic acid stimulates it 12. In his opinion, estrogen worsens the picture by releasing arachidonic acid, something progesterone curbs 11.

Inflammation, cell death and the debris of ageing

Peat believed that talking about inflammation explains nothing in itself: what matters is seeing what happens in the structure of the cell. For him, pain, excitation, inflammation and ageing share excitotoxic damage and an inability to produce or supply enough energy. He pointed out that the ageing brain has more carbon monoxide than at any earlier stage, and he considered this a degenerative component, not a protective one 13.

He also held that an important factor in ageing is interference with the repair, or the quick death, of damaged cells, which then can't be replaced from stem cells. In old age, according to him, that dying process is delayed, the cells can't make up their minds and the organism slows down, generating undesirable products such as lactic acid 14.

Cholesterol, plaque and protecting the brain

Peat stated that with age the free cholesterol in the brain decreases, because it binds to polyunsaturated fats that make it toxic, and that this lowers metabolism and brain function. According to him, plaque depends on the PUFA bound to cholesterol, which is why he considered it strictly a matter of excess PUFA, with lipofuscin present in arterial plaques 15.

Regarding sleep, he commented that it is an example of protective, restorative inhibition, regulated by the cell's energy charge. Since brain glycogen stores decrease with age, he interpreted this to mean that the protective inhibition weakens as we get older 16.

Frequently asked questions

What causes ageing according to Ray Peat?
According to Peat, degenerative ageing is produced by circumstances, especially by a contamination of the energy supply: unsaturated fats, excess iron and a lack of oxygen or usable energy 1. He also linked it to the progressive fall in oxygen consumption and metabolism 53.
Did Peat think ageing is programmed in the genes?
No. He held that the somatic mutation theory was shown to be false decades ago 2 and that the Hayflick limit depends on the cell's environment 4. For him, what limits us is our environment, which can be changed 2.
Does a fast metabolism shorten life according to Ray Peat?
For Peat it is exactly the opposite. He argued that the wear-and-tear and rate-of-living theories have been refuted, because more intense mitochondrial respiration reduces cell damage and favours a longer life 8. He cited as examples hamsters and mice with a high metabolism that lived longer 8.
What is lipofuscin and why did it concern Peat?
According to Peat, lipofuscin is an age pigment formed from PUFA breakdown products together with other substances. He held that it wastes oxygen and energy and can end up killing the cell 9. He added that stress and estrogen favour its formation 109.
