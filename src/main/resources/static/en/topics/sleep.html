Ray Peat on sleep

For Ray Peat, sleeping well depends above all on having enough cellular energy, something largely regulated by the thyroid, according to him 12. He held that darkness is a stress factor and that sleep serves largely to cushion it, so insomnia reflects an organism that can't relax for lack of energy 23.

Thyroid as the key hormone of sleep

When asked which hormones are lacking in people with insomnia, Peat answered that the main one is thyroid. He recalled that some sleep researchers had observed that certain people never got past the second of the four stages of sleep, and that people with a thyroid deficit went through the night without reaching deep sleep 1.

He also told how a very small dose of triiodothyronine (T3) worked for him to fall asleep after a whole day of driving, and that he had seen similar effects in other people 1. In his explanation, hypothyroidism is characterised by high levels of noradrenaline, serotonin, cortisol and estrogen, and that combination pushes the brain towards a state resembling torpor instead of restorative rest 2.

Darkness as stress, and night-time cortisol

Peat considered darkness one of the great stress factors for humans. He cited experiments with rabbits in Leningrad in which the mitochondria began to swell as soon as the light was turned off and, by three or four in the morning, collapsed and stopped producing energy 34.

He also mentioned a study in people: when the light was turned off, cortisol rose within a few minutes, but it did so much more slowly in those who fell asleep than in those who stayed awake. From this he concluded that sleeping is a way of reducing the need for cortisol 3. According to him, cortisol protects us from inflammatory processes that develop during the night, which is why growth hormone, adrenaline, cortisol and prolactin tend to rise while we sleep, while thyroid and progesterone fall 54.

Body temperature, torpor and deep sleep

For Peat, the torpor of hibernating animals is the opposite of restorative sleep. He pointed out that hibernating animals periodically come out of that state to really sleep, and that they then have a lot of deep, slow-wave sleep 2. He argued that hypothyroidism, old age and depression resemble each other in a sleep pattern with less deep sleep, similar to the one serotonin produces 2.

He added that body temperature regulates all bodily functions, including brain waves, and that it is usually overlooked 2. According to him, raising the temperature improves sleep, and a hot bath or even warming the feet would have an effect comparable to that of thyroid 2.

Salt, sugar and adrenaline at night

Peat held that salty and sugary foods before going to bed or during the night help you sleep better and longer, because both salt and sugar reduce adrenaline and tend to raise body temperature 2. He explained that hypothyroidism leaves the blood short of sodium and glucose, and that salty carbohydrates partly make up for that deficit 2. He related the sleep-inducing effect of salty food to the calming action of hyperosmolarity, of good thyroid function and of carbon dioxide 6.

He told how, after he suggested to several women that they salt their food to taste for premenstrual syndrome, many noticed they slept better, and the same happened with other people with sleep problems, whatever their age. He also cited a study in which most elderly people on a low-sodium diet suffered insomnia and had high adrenaline, and both returned to normal when they ate salt normally 7.

Milk, sugar, broth and gelatin in the small hours

Peat explained that what you take at bedtime or in the middle of the night can keep adrenaline and cortisol lower. For people with sleep problems he suggested juice, milk with sugar or a good, well-salted chicken broth, and pointed out that taking fluid helps keep the blood from thickening 5. He said that he himself slept between seven and a half and nine hours, that he usually woke up and had milk with sugar or juice, and that this helped him avoid so much stress at dawn. He preferred white sugar because, according to him, honey can cause allergic reactions and heating maple sugar makes it allergenic 8.

He had also long recommended a gelatinous broth before sleeping to curb night-time stress. He told how, after years of not getting through the night without waking, he slept about nine hours straight the first time he took several grams of gelatin just before going to bed 9. He linked this to the fact that, in his view, the excess cysteine and tryptophan in muscle meat have antithyroid effects 9.

Ageing, relaxation and dreams

Peat observed that babies sleep a great deal, have a very high metabolism and a stable temperature, while with age metabolism falls and sleep time shrinks by about thirty minutes per decade after middle age 2. For him, insomnia in older people and the heart's difficulty relaxing between beats are the same thing: when energy production falls, both the heart and the brain lose their capacity to relax 10. He gave progesterone a favourable role, because it promotes a state of high-energy rest in nerve cells and raises the temperature set point 2.

On dreams, he thought they appear mainly towards the end of the night, when cortisol peaks at dawn, and that they could be a defensive response of the brain in that more stressful phase. He associated them with deep muscle relaxation and, probably, with restorative actions on the nerves 11.
