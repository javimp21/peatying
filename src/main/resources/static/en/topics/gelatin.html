Ray Peat on gelatin

For Ray Peat, gelatin was an especially protective protein because it provides a lot of glycine and proline, with anti-inflammatory and cell-stabilising effects 12. It also lacks tryptophan and has very little cysteine and methionine, amino acids that, according to him, slow the thyroid, promote inflammation and shorten life 31.

What gelatin is, according to Peat

Peat explained that gelatin is the cooked form of collagen, the structural protein that makes adult animal bodies tough. According to him, collagen makes up about half of the protein in an animal's body 13. Its main amino acids are glycine and proline, with a notable amount of alanine 1.

He also pointed out that many people sell gelatin as if it were collagen, when it isn't: collagen is the raw form present in tissues 4. Eating gelatin, he said, doesn't increase one's own collagen production, but serves to avoid unnecessary stimulation of tissue growth 4.

What it doesn't contain: tryptophan, cysteine and methionine

For Peat, the great value of gelatin lay in what it lacks. It has no tryptophan and only small amounts of cysteine, methionine and histidine 1. He held that those amino acids, very abundant in muscle meat, inhibit thyroid function, slow metabolism and promote inflammation 3.

He cited animal experiments in which removing methionine alone extended maximum lifespan by around 40% 53. He added that an adult needs very little of these amino acids, because they serve mainly for growth, which is why gelatin seemed to him a protective protein for a mature person 23. He recalled that decades ago nutritionists were amazed that healthy elderly women lived almost on gelatin and toast 3.

He also stated that tryptophan and cysteine become more toxic when they are dehydrated and exposed to oxygen, and that almost all protein powders degrade in this way. Gelatin, as far as he knew, is uniquely stable against dehydration 2.

Glycine as a calming amino acid

According to Peat, a good supply of glycine within a balanced set of amino acids has many anti-stress actions. He described it as an inhibitory neurotransmitter that favours natural sleep 1. For him, it has a calming and protective action in all kinds of cells, and the damage caused by excess tryptophan and serotonin seemed to be prevented or corrected by it 1.

He told how a small dose of glycine taken shortly after a stroke sped up recovery, and that its nerve-stabilising effect is also protective in epilepsy 1. He added that glycine and alanine seemed to protect without needing to be metabolised, simply through their physical properties 1.

Inflammation, fibrosis and healing

Peat argued that the relief of arthritis with gelatin isn't mainly due to repairing cartilage. Some of it is incorporated into cartilage, but slowly, while relief of pain and inflammation can be almost immediate, similar to that of cortisol or aspirin 1. He explained that inflammation produces fibrosis and that, in the lungs, kidneys, liver and other tissues, glycine protects against it, contrary to what the traditional idea would suggest 1.

He also noted that when collagen breaks down, factors are released that favour healing and curb tumour invasion, and that glycine itself is one of them 1. He attributed a wide range of antitumour actions to it, including inhibiting the formation of new blood vessels 1. He also mentioned that a 1936 medical pharmacopoeia used gelatin solutions to control various kinds of bleeding 1.

Muscle meat versus the whole animal

Peat held that eating only muscle meat produces in the blood a balance of amino acids like that of extreme stress, in which excess cortisol breaks down muscle. Excess tryptophan increases serotonin, which stimulates more cortisol, while the tryptophan and cysteine in muscle suppress the thyroid 1.

That is why he valued traditional ways of eating the whole animal, such as chicken foot soup, pig's head or fish head soup, which provide a lot of glycine and gelatin 1. He observed that people who eat the tough parts, such as feet and wings, get much more gelatin than those who eat tender steaks 5. A gelatinous chicken soup seemed to him a traditional remedy for the sick 4.

How and how much he used it, according to his writings

Peat considered that a person eating a large serving of meat benefits from taking 5 or 10 grams of gelatin at the same time, so that the amino acids reach the blood in balance 1. He said powdered gelatin needs no cooking, although dissolving it in hot water speeds digestion somewhat, and that it can be added to custards, mousses, soups, sauces or juice desserts 1. As for quantity, he stated that it is safe even in large doses 5 and considered about 100 grams a day reasonable for an adult, although in a growing child it would displace other important foods 1.

He preferred gelatin to pure glycine because some cell protections are achieved almost equally with alanine and proline, and because gelatin comes with its natural associated substances 1. So isolated glycine, although he saw it as a useful and very safe drug, shouldn't be considered a food, since manufactured products can contain contaminants 1.

Frequently asked questions

Are gelatin and collagen the same according to Ray Peat?
No. Peat explained that collagen is the raw form that exists in tissues and that gelatin is its cooked form 41. He criticised selling gelatin as collagen and pointed out that genuine purified collagen is very expensive 4.
Why did Peat prefer gelatin to meat as a protein?
Because gelatin lacks tryptophan and has very little cysteine and methionine, amino acids that, according to him, inhibit the thyroid and promote inflammation and ageing 31. He held that eating only muscle reproduces the amino acid balance of stress, and that gelatin helps offset it 1.
What did Peat say about gelatin and arthritis?
He thought its effect isn't mainly due to rebuilding cartilage, since that incorporation is slow 1. He attributed the rapid relief of pain and inflammation to an anti-inflammatory action similar to that of cortisol or aspirin 1.
Why does glycine help sleep according to Peat?
Peat described it as an inhibitory neurotransmitter that favours natural sleep 1. He explained that it stabilises nerves, so more stimulation is needed to activate them, an important effect in regulating sleep, breathing and heart rhythm 1.
