Ray Peat on vitamins

For Ray Peat, vitamins were not mere supplements: he saw them as tied to hormones, to polyunsaturated fats and to the energy metabolism of cells. He held that vitamin E is above all an estrogen antagonist and not just an antioxidant 1, and that others such as A, D, K, niacinamide and riboflavin play a part in the thyroid, calcium and mitochondrial respiration 234.

Vitamin E as an estrogen antagonist

According to Peat, estrogen increases prostaglandin synthesis and activates enzymes such as COX and LOX, while vitamin E does the opposite 1. He also held that estrogen releases enzymes from lysosomes and that vitamin E restrains that release 1.

Polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA) and prostaglandins act, in his view, as amplifiers of estrogen's effects. So he thought that if vitamin E protects against those fats, that alone would explain part of its antiestrogenic effect 1. He added that it inhibits lipolysis and binds free fatty acids to inactivate them 1.

Vitamin E, fertility and polyunsaturated fats

Peat recounted that his thesis supervisor found in hamsters that extra vitamin E delayed middle-age infertility 1. In his own experiments, vitamin E increased oxygen in the uterus and corrected the deficit caused by added estrogen or by old age 1. According to him, estrogen reduces the oxygen available to the embryo and vitamin E increases it 1.

He pointed out that the need for vitamin E grows with age and with the amount of polyunsaturated fat in the diet. If those fats are carefully avoided, the need would be very low 5. In rats, the amount needed to sustain pregnancy rose about 65-fold during the first year of life, and he believed estrogen raises the need for this vitamin 6.

Vitamin A, the thyroid and carotene

For Peat, vitamin A is the main cofactor that lets the thyroid turn cholesterol into steroid hormones 7. He said the two work so closely together that a single protein, transthyretin, carries both, and that without thyroid vitamin A cannot be used 2.

In that context, he stated that carotene accumulates in steroid-producing tissues in hypothyroidism and competes with vitamin A, having an effect opposite to it 72. He recalled that in the 1930s the red colour of those tissues was used to confirm death from hypothyroidism 2.

He also saw risks: he believed an excess of vitamin A inhibits the thyroid and that there is less risk of toxicity if vitamin E is taken at the same time 8. Both its deficiency and a large excess release enzymes that dissolve cartilage 8.

Vitamins D and K and calcium control

According to Peat, vitamin D or a high calcium intake switches off parathyroid hormone, and that would prevent calcification of soft tissues 9. He preferred sunlight to supplements 9. When he spoke of vitamin D he meant cholecalciferol, not the 1,25-hydroxylated form, which in his view does the dirty work of the parathyroid 3. He also held that vitamins D and K need carbon dioxide to handle calcium properly 10.

Of vitamin K2 he said it blocks the bone calcium loss caused by parathyroid hormone by preventing lactic acid production, and that it promotes the formation of bone proteins 11. He stated that proteins C and S, which prevent abnormal clotting, cannot be made without it 12. He added that much research supports it in hypertension, although in his view most doctors advise against it because of its reputation as a clotting vitamin 13. He mentioned cases of high doses lowering blood pressure and said that in Japan doses far above dietary amounts were used without problems 1214.

B vitamins: niacinamide and riboflavin

Peat favoured niacinamide because, according to him, it supplies energy to the mitochondrial system, reduces cell excitation and helps stabilise repair systems 4. He explained that estrogen interferes with the formation of niacin from tryptophan 4. He insisted on using the amide form and not nicotinic acid, because the flush it causes goes with the release of serotonin, histamine and toxic prostaglandins. He said 'no-flush' niacin releases the same things, only more slowly 1516.

Of riboflavin (B2) he said it is a component of the mitochondrial respiratory enzymes and is easily destroyed by light 4. He described the case of a child with nosebleeds and fits of rage that improved after a dose of riboflavin, and attributed it to an energy deficit 4. On doses, he criticised the excess of the orthomolecular movement and preferred to try the smallest possible dose first, around five times the minimum daily requirement 16.

His criticism of the official view of vitamins

Peat held that in the 1940s vitamin E was redefined as an antioxidant, and that this change diverted attention from its relationship with estrogen and unsaturated fats 51. According to him, the estrogen industry and the seed oil industry benefited from that idea, and research into its healing effects was held back for about 40 years 171.

He also stated that in the 1970s drug companies with synthetic forms of vitamin A promoted exaggerated stories about the toxicity of the natural one. That would have led many people to stop eating liver, which for him is an important source of vitamin E and vitamin K 1. Even so, he acknowledged that vitamins A and D can be toxic in large amounts 18.

Frequently asked questions

Did Ray Peat consider vitamin E an antioxidant?
According to Peat, that is a later redefinition: before the 1940s it was seen as an antiestrogen 5. For him, its main role is to counter the effects of estrogen and polyunsaturated fats, not just to protect against oxidation 1.
What link did Peat see between vitamin A and the thyroid?
He held that the two work very closely together: they share a transport protein and, without thyroid, vitamin A cannot be used 2. He also said that in hypothyroidism carotene accumulates and competes with vitamin A 72.
Why did Peat prefer niacinamide to nicotinic acid?
According to him, the flush from nicotinic acid comes with the release of serotonin, histamine and toxic prostaglandins, and 'no-flush' niacin releases the same things more slowly 15. For Peat, the amide form avoids that 16.
What did Peat think of vitamins D and K?
He believed vitamin D, together with calcium, keeps parathyroid hormone low, and that K2 protects bone against its action 911. He defended vitamin K against its reputation for promoting clots, because it is needed to make proteins C and S 12.
