Ray Peat on serotonin

According to Peat, serotonin is not the happiness substance but a mediator of stress, shock and inflammation that reduces circulation and energy production 1. He held that the opposite popular idea is a myth that arose from the 1970s onwards 2 and that excess serotonin contributes to many diseases 1.

A stress mediator, not the happiness hormone

Peat explained that the name serotonin refers to its effect on pressure: it increases the tone of blood vessels. According to him, in the 1950s it was identified as a cause of emotional disturbances, of blood pressure problems and of many circulatory problems 2. He placed the origin of the happy-hormone myth in the 1970s 2.

For him, the pharmaceutical industry spread the belief that serotonin is the chemistry of happiness and that tryptophan is its harmless precursor 1. He added that the current doctrine about serotonin, mood and behaviour didn't appear until the 1960s 3. He recognised that serotonin has its own physiological functions, but considered it a stress mediator that must be balanced by systems of high respiratory metabolism 3.

Cellular energy, temperature and metabolism

According to Peat, stress impairs metabolism and serotonin slows energy production in the mitochondria 1. He held that, like estrogen, it lowers cellular ATP and interferes with oxidative metabolism 3. He also said that it tends to cause hypoglycaemia, which in turn hinders the conversion of thyroxine into the active hormone T3 3.

He linked serotonin to lethargy and hibernation: in hibernating animals, food shortage increases its production, and in humans the stress of winter causes similar changes 3. For him, serotonin lowers temperature by reducing the metabolic rate, and in winter more thyroid hormone would be needed to keep metabolism normal 3. He added that hypothyroidism increases serotonin activity in the body, just as it increases estrogen dominance 1.

Gut, cortisol and inflammation

Peat stated that about 95% of the body's serotonin comes from the gut, where it and histamine are produced in large amounts under stress 4. According to him, its rise in the digestive tract encourages endotoxin and nitric oxide to rise, which harms respiratory energy and shifts metabolism towards lactic acid 4. That lactic acid would activate stress hormones and promote inflammation 4.

For him, serotonin is one of the most powerful activators of the cortisol system, because it acts on the hypothalamus, the pituitary and other tissues 4. He linked its excess to stomach ulcers and to cancers of the stomach and intestine 4. He also held that it activates the glucocorticoid system, which can produce brain atrophy 5, and that stress makes the gut absorb more endotoxin, which releases serotonin from the platelets 1.

Brain, learning and behaviour

According to Peat, excess serotonin interferes with the consolidation of learning, and hypothermia has a similar effect. He used this to explain estrogen's interference with learning, since estrogen raises serotonin and lowers body temperature 3. He said that antiserotonin agents protect against brain atrophy and other effects of stress 5.

He described a harm-avoidance personality trait, described by Cloninger and associated with higher serotonin, that leads people to avoid danger instead of acting creatively 6. On aggression, he acknowledged that nobody knows the mechanism, although he pointed out that it can be suppressed with inescapable stress, in which serotonin seems to be the decisive piece 6. In an interview he added that authoritarianism and isolation trigger an excess of serotonin that cools the body and pushes towards passivity and learned helplessness 7.

SSRI antidepressants and tryptophan supplements

Peat questioned whether selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) work by raising serotonin. He told how in dogs treated with one of them for a month, serotonin had gone down, and he believed that the antidepressants that work shift the balance away from serotonin in the long run 8. According to him, injecting serotonin never relieves depression, whereas substances that antagonise it do seem to act as antidepressants 3.

He also pointed out that these drugs have other effects, such as increasing allopregnanolone in the brain, which mimics progesterone 3. He considered the use of tryptophan or hydroxytryptophan supplements, or drugs that promote serotonin, biologically inappropriate 3. He held that tryptophan, its precursor, is carcinogenic or tumour-promoting, and that an overdose of SSRIs or hydroxytryptophan can cause serotonin syndrome 13.

What would counteract it, and where it comes from

For Peat, thyroid, progesterone and high-quality protein are central to protecting against syndromes of excess serotonin 3. He said that progesterone can inhibit the firing of serotonergic neurons 3 and that salt helps counteract excess serotonin by reducing stress hormones, especially adrenaline 2. He added that branched-chain amino acids have some antiserotonin activity, because they compete with tryptophan to enter cells 3.

On diet, he explained that a high-carbohydrate meal raises the ratio of tryptophan to competing amino acids 3 and that excess dietary tryptophan can add to inflammation and increase serotonin 9. According to him, adults need very little tryptophan, and meat is another excessive source 10. He also pointed out that bananas, pineapples and tomatoes contain enough serotonin to affect susceptible people, and that polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA) favour the entry of tryptophan into the brain and its conversion into serotonin 9.

Frequently asked questions

Is serotonin the happiness hormone according to Ray Peat?
No. For Peat it was a myth that began to spread in the 1970s, and he recalled that in the 1950s it was associated with emotional and circulatory disorders 2. He held that it is a mediator of stress and inflammation, not a well-being substance 1.
What did Ray Peat think of SSRI antidepressants?
He doubted that they work by increasing serotonin and cited a case of dogs in which, after a month of treatment, serotonin had decreased 8. For him, injecting serotonin never relieves depression, and the effect of these drugs could be explained by their action on other amines 3.
Where does the body's serotonin come from according to Peat?
According to Peat, about 95% of the body's serotonin is produced in the gut, especially under stress 4. He added that dietary tryptophan is its precursor and that an excess, together with inflammation, can increase it 9.
What link did Peat see between serotonin and the thyroid?
He held that hypothyroidism increases serotonin activity 1 and that many symptoms of excess serotonin overlap with those of hypothyroidism 3. That is why he considered the thyroid, together with progesterone and good-quality protein, central to protecting against its effects 3.
