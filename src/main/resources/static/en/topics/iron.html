Ray Peat on iron

According to Peat, iron is a potentially toxic heavy metal that, in excess, can contribute to cancer, heart disease and other illnesses 1. He held that the usual diet provides more iron than is needed and that official recommendations should be revised well downwards 21. For him, moreover, anaemia doesn't automatically mean iron deficiency 1.

Why he saw iron as a toxic metal

Peat compared stored iron with other heavy metals, such as lead, mercury, cadmium or nickel, because it too generates destructive free radicals. According to him, the effects of those radicals are practically indistinguishable from those of X-rays and gamma rays, and they speed up the build-up of age pigment and other signs of ageing 1. For him, excess iron is key to stress ending up as tissue damage 1.

In support, he cited that in the 1960s the World Health Organization saw a large increase in deaths from infectious diseases, especially malaria, when iron was given to anaemic people in Africa. He also pointed out that regulating iron is a central function of the immune system, because bacteria, parasites and cancer cells need it to grow 1. He added that, according to recent studies, excess iron is involved in degenerative brain diseases such as Parkinson's or Alzheimer's, and that it is credited with a role in skin ageing, arteriosclerosis and cataracts 1.

Iron, polyunsaturated fats and reductive stress

According to Peat, iron and polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA) start causing problems together around the age of 45 or 50. He explained that PUFA tend to be stored while the body burns saturated fats first, so after decades on an average diet the tissues are heavily loaded with PUFA. At the same time, on a typical Western diet, the liver and bone marrow become overloaded with iron 2.

For him, the chemical form of iron is what matters: the reduced (ferrous) form is the reactive one, and it is activated when tissues lack oxygen. That is why he spoke of reductive stress rather than oxidative stress, and gave alcohol as an example of something that pushes the balance towards reduction 3. Under those conditions, he held, iron sets off chain reactions of inflammation that damage the mitochondria and push the cell towards glycolysis with lactic acid production 2.

Anaemia doesn't always mean a lack of iron

Peat recalled that a century ago arsenic was prescribed for anaemia and that it worked, because it stimulated the formation of red blood cells. For him, that doesn't show a deficiency of arsenic or of iron: the body responds with more blood production to various harmful factors, and iron acts just like arsenic 13. That is why he thought an improvement after taking supplements doesn't prove that iron was lacking 3.

He held that you can be anaemic and at the same time have too much iron 1. He considered that iron-deficiency anaemia exists, for example in some cases of chronic bleeding, but that it should be the last cause suspected, not the first, and only with specific tests of iron saturation in certain proteins 1. He added that he knew of no blood test that properly reported iron stores, because iron hides in the liver and bone marrow 3. In a specific situation such as a large blood loss in surgery, he believed liver provides as much iron as a pill and is safer 1.

Women, estrogen and thyroid

Peat denied that women need more iron than men. According to him, estrogen gives them a great advantage in absorbing it, several times greater than a man's, and also favours the conditions that make iron toxic 321. He went as far as saying that estrogen creates the illusion of a lack of oxygen so that more iron is absorbed 3.

He pointed out that menstruation only loses a few milligrams a day and that the hard part is avoiding iron, not getting it 1. According to him, by 65 women accumulate as much iron as men, and their risk of dying of a heart attack becomes the same 1. To explain apparent anaemia in women he pointed to low thyroid function, which cools the arms and legs and slows the production of red blood cells. He estimated that more than half of the iron deficiencies diagnosed in women would actually be thyroid deficiencies, and added that high estrogen also gives the appearance of mild anaemia 42. In that sense, he said that estrogen dilutes the blood, so it is normal for women to have less haemoglobin 1.

Children and pregnancy: his warnings about iron

Peat mentioned some researchers' concern about the iron added to cereals and its possible link to childhood leukaemia and lymphatic cancers. He explained that children are born with large stores, that these decrease while they grow, and that storage increases after puberty 1. He also cited a study of the “malnourished” children of Californian farm workers: those with severe anaemia resisted infections better than well-fed middle-class children 1.

On pregnancy, he held that since iron supplements became widespread, newborn jaundice has increased. He suggested it could be due to iron poisoning and that those supplements could also play a part in the rise of childhood cancer 1. As for prenatal supplements, he commented that they traditionally emphasise iron, something he didn't agree with 2.

Copper, vitamin E and habits that, according to him, protect

For Peat, iron and copper influence each other: too much iron blocks the absorption of copper, and too little copper makes the body store too much iron. With age, he said, tissues lose copper while they accumulate iron 1. That is why he proposed regularly eating foods rich in copper, such as shrimp and oysters 1.

Since iron destroys vitamin E, he suggested taking about 100 mg of this vitamin a day, preferably away from iron-rich meals, and considered that the need grows with age 1. He also said that coffee with meals strongly inhibits iron absorption, which is why he drank it with meat, and that vitamin C increases it, so he preferred not to drink orange juice with iron-rich foods. He added that cutting down on unsaturated fats makes iron less harmful 1.

Frequently asked questions

Why did Ray Peat consider iron dangerous?
According to Peat, stored iron produces destructive free radicals, like other heavy metals, and speeds up cellular ageing 1. He held that in excess it favours infections, degenerative brain diseases, arteriosclerosis and cataracts 1. He also linked it to polyunsaturated fats, which accumulate in the tissues over the years 2.
Did Peat think anaemia is the same as iron deficiency?
No. For him, anaemia doesn't necessarily imply a nutritional need for iron, and there can even be excess iron with anaemia 1. He argued that iron deficiency should be the last cause suspected, and that the real cause is often low thyroid function 12.
Do women need more iron than men according to Peat?
According to Peat, that is a misunderstanding: estrogen makes women absorb iron very efficiently, and menstruation only means a loss of a few milligrams a day 21. He added that by around 65 they accumulate as much iron as men 1.
What link did Peat see between iron, copper and vitamin E?
He held that excess iron hinders the absorption of copper and that a lack of copper makes the body retain more iron 1. He also said that iron destroys vitamin E, so the need for it increases with the iron accumulated over a lifetime 1.
