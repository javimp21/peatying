Ray Peat on the thyroid

For Ray Peat, thyroid function is the basis of energy metabolism: according to him, a low thyroid is better recognised by temperature, pulse and energy use than by a single blood test 12. He held that stress hormones and TSH can give a misleading picture of what is really happening in the body 13. He also argued that T3 is the truly active form and that the natural balance between T4 and T3 matters 45.

Temperature and pulse as signals

Peat recalled that Broda Barnes advocated measuring the temperature on waking, before getting up, as the best basis for assessing the thyroid 1. Barnes expected a morning temperature of around 97.8 °F (36.6 °C) rising during the day to about 98.6 °F (37 °C) 6. For Peat, the ideal test to prove hypothyroidism was still the measurement of oxygen consumption, and temperature and pulse made it possible to get quite close to it 2.

Peat warned that temperature alone can be misleading. In the hot, humid summer of Eugene he saw people with clear symptoms of hypothyroidism and a normal temperature, but a very low pulse, because keeping the body at that temperature costs very little energy when it is hot 61. That is why he advocated combining both readings, which according to him work much better together than separately 1. He also asked whether the person used an electric blanket and, if so, discarded the measurement 1.

Adrenaline, cortisol and readings through the day

According to Peat, many people with a low thyroid compensate with a lot of adrenaline, which speeds up the pulse, and with it cortisol rises and raises the waking temperature 12. So a single morning reading can be ambiguous. He proposed repeating it one or two hours after eating, because food and daylight lower adrenaline and cortisol 2.

In his experience, someone who woke with a pulse of 75 and 98 °F (36.7 °C) could drop to a pulse of 55 or 60 and 96-97 °F (35.6-36.1 °C) by mid-morning, which for him indicated a very low thyroid 2. He added that counting calories works in a similar way to measuring oxygen, and that eating around 1800 calories a day with a stable weight made a diagnosis of hyperthyroidism hard to believe, in his view 1.

Why he distrusted TSH

Peat criticised the way TSH, the pituitary hormone that stimulates the thyroid, is interpreted as if it had meaning on its own. According to him, it can fall into the normal range or below it for reasons unrelated to thyroid hormones 1. Stress, estrogen, cortisol and adrenaline lower it and, in his view, make it look as if there is too much thyroid hormone when there is actually too little 3. That is why he thought it should carry only moderate weight when interpreting thyroid function 1.

He also held that TSH acts on many types of cells and probably contributes to oedema and fibrosis 1. He pointed out that animals without a pituitary, or dwarf mice without TSH, lived longer than normal, and that he saw no reason to set a lower limit of normality 1. He recommended relying on other tests, such as the Achilles reflex, the electrocardiogram, or measurements of cholesterol, albumin, glucose, sodium and lactate 1.

Stress slows the conversion of T4 into T3

According to Peat, cortisol inhibits the conversion of T4 into T3, which is responsible for producing respiratory energy and carbon dioxide 1. Adrenaline also increases reverse T3, which acts as an antagonist 1. For him, all stress hormones interfere with active T3 in this way and generate an inactive substance that slows cellular respiration 3.

He also argued that adrenaline releases fatty acids which, if they are polyunsaturated (PUFA), inhibit the production and transport of thyroid hormone and damage the respiration of mitochondria 1. For him there was a vicious circle: stress causes hypothyroidism and hypothyroidism causes stress 3. He described hypothyroidism with high levels of noradrenaline, serotonin, cortisol and estrogen 7.

T3, T4 and natural thyroid

Peat explained that T4 is only active after being converted into T3, which is what activates the heat-producing oxidative process 5. T4 takes weeks to stabilise in the body, with a half-life of about two weeks, while T3 lasts about twelve hours or a day 5. Even so, T4 can have an effect on the pituitary and lower TSH, although that doesn't amount to increasing heat production 5.

For him, an excess of T4 in a tissue that doesn't convert it quickly has an antithyroid effect, and he cited as an example brains whose respiration was reduced by T4 4. He told of a woman whose thyroxine dose kept being raised while she got worse, until she fell into a myxoedema coma, and who recovered quickly with T3 8. He also held that the healthy gland releases a lot of T3 relative to T4, and that a natural gland preparation is closer to that balance than thyroxine alone 4. He added that the brain concentrates T3 and that many people say they think more clearly with a ratio close to three parts T4 to one part T3 4.

Other effects he attributed to the thyroid

Peat stated that when the thyroid is low, clotting increases, and that venous thrombosis, transient ischaemic attacks and strokes generally resolve with a thyroid supplement 5. He also said that hypofunction is an important cause of high blood pressure 1. On the fear that taking thyroid shuts down one's own gland, he held that isotope studies showed the gland started working again a few days after stopping the dose 9.

In his view of cancer, the thyroid keeps the cell in an oxidative state, and the carbon dioxide it produces prevents the formation of toxic lactic acid and free radicals 10. He also saw winter and darkness as a stress that tends to suppress thyroid function and favours the accumulation of estrogen because of a sluggish liver 11.

Frequently asked questions

How did Ray Peat detect a low thyroid?
According to Peat, the best practical approach was to measure temperature and pulse on waking and again one or two hours after eating 2. He considered the combination of both readings much more reliable than either on its own 1. For him, the ideal test was still oxygen consumption 2.
Why did Peat say TSH is unreliable?
He held that TSH can fall because of stress, estrogen, cortisol or adrenaline, and not only because of excess thyroid hormone 13. So, in his view, a low TSH can give the false impression of hyperthyroidism in someone who actually has a slow metabolism 3. He thought it should carry only moderate weight when assessing the thyroid 1.
What difference did Peat see between T4 and T3?
For him, T4 is only active when it is converted into T3, which is what stimulates oxidative metabolism and heat production 5. He held that excess T4 in tissues that don't convert it well can have an antithyroid effect 4. That is why he preferred a ratio similar to the one the healthy gland releases, with more T3 than thyroxine alone provides 4.
What link did Peat see between stress and the thyroid?
According to Peat, stress hormones slow the conversion of T4 into T3 and favour reverse T3, which blocks cellular respiration 13. He argued that hypothyroidism causes stress and that stress, in turn, worsens hypothyroidism 3. He added that winter darkness is a stress that tends to suppress the thyroid 11.
